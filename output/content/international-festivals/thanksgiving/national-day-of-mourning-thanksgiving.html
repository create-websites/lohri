National Day of Mourning: Indigenous Perspectives on Thanksgiving

Learn about the National Day of Mourning in Plymouth, its roots in 1970, and the reasons why Indigenous individuals utilize Thanksgiving for reflection and advocacy.

Read the guideAll 20 articles
🍁 ThanksgivingHistory, Heritage & Perspectives

A different meaning on the same day

Since 1970, the United American Indians of New England (UAINE) has been hosting a National Day of Mourning in Plymouth, Massachusetts, on U.S. Thanksgiving. The event is dedicated to honoring Indigenous ancestors and their strength, reflecting on the impacts of colonization, and speaking out against continued racism and oppression.

This tradition coexists with, rather than simply mirrors, the family-focused Thanksgiving observed by numerous Americans, showcasing the contrasting historical significance that one national holiday can hold.

How the observance began

UAINE links the inaugural National Day of Mourning to Wamsutta Frank James, an Aquinnah Wampanoag chief invited to address a 1970 event marking the Pilgrims’ landing. However, event planners disapproved of his intended speech, which questioned traditional narratives of colonialism.

The protest evolved into a yearly Indigenous-led event at Cole’s Hill in Plymouth.

Why language and attribution matter

In discussing the National Day of Mourning, it is crucial to credit the individuals and groups sharing their perspectives. UAINE views Thanksgiving as a stark reminder of genocide, land theft, and cultural erasure for numerous Native peoples. This is the perspective they have expressed and the focus of their observance.

Do not generalize all Indigenous nations as sharing one viewpoint. Indigenous communities have a range of perspectives on Thanksgiving.

What respectful learning can look like

Families and schools are encouraged to combine gratitude practices with learning about local history by discovering whose homelands they reside on, reading material authored by Native Americans, supporting modern Native organizations, and avoiding stereotypical "Pilgrim and Indian" costumes. The aim is to promote historical literacy, not engage in guilt theater or romanticization.

Frequently asked questions

When did the National Day of Mourning begin?

UAINE has organized the observance in Plymouth since 1970.

Do all Native Americans view Thanksgiving in the same way?

Indigenous nations and individuals are a diverse group, with the National Day of Mourning reflecting specific Indigenous traditions and perspectives rather than a universal Native opinion.
