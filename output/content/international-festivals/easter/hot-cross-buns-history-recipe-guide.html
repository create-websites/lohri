Hot Cross Buns: History, Tradition and Baking Guide

Discover the history behind why spiced cross-marked buns are tied to Good Friday and master baking a fluffy, aromatic batch in your own kitchen.

Read the guideAll 20 articles
🌼 EasterCulinary & Baking Trends

Why the buns are tied to Holy Week

Hot cross buns, commonly linked with Good Friday in Britain and other regions with British culinary influences, are transformed into a symbol of Holy Week with their spiced dough, dried fruit, and signature cross decoration.

Build flavor before baking

Make sure to use a yeast dough that is lightly enriched with warm spices like cinnamon and nutmeg, along with sweet additions like raisins, currants, or chopped dried fruit. Allow the dough to rise completely to ensure that the buns turn out light and fluffy instead of heavy.

Make the cross and finish

Before baking, you can pipe a basic mixture of flour and water over the formed buns. Once baked, give them a glossy finish by brushing on a light glaze like warmed apricot jam or simple syrup.

Serve fresh or revive gently

Freshly baked hot cross buns are most delicious on the day they are made, but they also make a tasty breakfast when toasted the following morning. Enjoy them with butter or save older buns to use in bread pudding or a baked breakfast casserole.

Frequently asked questions

Are hot cross buns only eaten on Good Friday?

Although Good Friday is their primary traditional connection, bakeries commonly offer them for sale during Lent and the Easter season.

What makes the cross on top?

A common practice in modern recipes is to pipe a mixture of flour and water before baking, while other traditions prefer using icing after baking.
